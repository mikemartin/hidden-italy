        text: "Milan's cemetery is a gothic masterpiece that captures the city's exuberant sense of itself. Milan is an unprepossessing city: an industrial sprawl that spreads like a stain across the fertile plains of the Po River in northern Italy. It is essentially a modern city, which lacks the charm of the more romantic Venice, Florence or Rome. But as anyone who has walked through the shopping precincts around Via Montenapoleone, the shops of Corso Vittorio Emanuele and the Galleria, or the studios and cafes of Brera will testify, the Milanese have a certain style. So much so, in fact, that they can make even the most sophisticated visitor feel like a provincial.\_"

        text: "This ability is not accidental. Nor is it something innate. It is a skill that all Milanese learn from a very early age. \"La bella figura\", the gentle art of impressing those around you, is a Milanese obsession and a defining quality of these industrious people. Milan has been Italy's economic powerhouse for well over a century, luring the ambitious and upwardly-mobile from all over the country. Display, one-upping your neighbours, is a natural corollary to hard-earned success, and it is a pre-occupation that the Milanese take to their graves.\_"

        text: "The Cimitero Monumentale (the Monumental Cemetery) was built and largely filled during the heroic period of Milanese capitalism, at the end of the 19th century and the beginning of the 20th century. Where Milanese style is generally impeccable (a seductive blend of Mediterranean sensuality with classic proportion and lines), occasionally it is not. The Cimitero is a fine example of this, where the exuberance of newfound riches overwhelmed the sobriety and good taste of these northern Italians. \_Only a short tram ride from the centre, the cemetery is 25 hectares of sometimes the most surprising kitsch, holding the work of some of Milan's finest modern sculptures including Luca Beltrami, Medaro Rosso, Giacomo Manzu and Gio Pomodoro, and is well worth a visit.\_"

        text: 'Behind a vast gravel car park studded with flower stalls rises a row of towering chapels built in layers of black and white marble, an example of "modern Lombard Gothic". It is dominated by the Famedio, the central chapel, which is reached by a flight of broad marble stairs. It was conceived as the Milanese Pantheon and contains the remains of the city''s most illustrious citizens. Passing under its octagonal cupola, you arrive at another enormous flight of stairs, and, spreading below you, a breathtaking view of the vast necropolis. Avenues of pines and yew trees are crowded with rows upon rows of graves and massive family vaults.'

        text: "While the vaults speak of power and wealth, the graves, and their sculptures, are often more personal and intimate, many speaking of the horror of death. Over one, the Grim Reaper, two metres tall and cast in blackened bronze, his arms stretched in front of him like a sleepwalker, advances on the visitor out of the shadows of a pine tree, every bit as scary as Boris Karloff. Above another, the beautiful torso of a youthful bare-breasted woman emerges from the grave, struggling to escape her fate. All in vain, as behind her the wizened hand of a leering old man catches her shoulder and draws her back. Another shows a young man killed in a car crash, who appears to be doing a swirling handstand on his grave, but is in fact clutching a steering wheel, which is drawing him to the netherworld. Yet another shows a handsome young man killed in Mussolini's air force, who is naked except for a pilot's cap, clasping a full-size replica of a plane's propeller and being pulled down to Hades by an ugly-headed Medusa, snakes writhing around his ankles."

        text: "Other graves speak of sorrow and loss, showing life-sized figures of mothers and wives draped in mourning across the graves of their loved ones. The saddest, at the far end of the cemetery, is also one of the simplest. It features an immaculately maintained lush green lawn, in the middle of which stands the perfect replica of a two-year-old child searching for a lost toy. While the graves are fascinating, it is the vaults that most reveal the Milanese spirit. These massive structures were built not simply to defy mortality, but to establish their families' status for eternity. The Campari vault, for example, stands on a plinth almost two metres high, topped by a life-size, bronze reproduction of Leonardo da Vinci's Last Supper. While the face of Christ is that of Leonardo's mural, the faces of the Apostles are those of the Campari family. Only slightly less ambitious is the Ustigio tomb, a six-metre-high pyramid, complete with hieroglyphics. The entrance to the underground vault is guarded by a sphinx. Opposite this, in Egyptian garb, is a life-sized sculpture of the dearly departed welcoming the visitor and the new arrivals. Reference to ancient times is a recurrent theme. Other vaults include huge scale replicas of the Parthenon and the tower of Babel."

        type: text
        text: "However, it is the Besenzanica Tomb, with its Allegory of Work by Enrico Butti, that most captures the heroic aspirations of the Milanese entrepreneurs at the turn of the century. In life-size realism, it recreates a mountain peak. The deceased is portrayed as a peasant, mounted on a plough. The monstrous head of Aeolius, the god of wind, is peering over the edge and blowing a gale upon him. Undaunted, with flaying whip in hand, the deceased is driving a full team of oxen victoriously to the crest.\_ A bella figura indeed!"
