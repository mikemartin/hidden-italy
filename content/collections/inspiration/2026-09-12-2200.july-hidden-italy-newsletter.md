        text: 'Garfagnana is a mountainous district in the north-west corner of Tuscany, sandwiched between the rugged peaks and marble, quarries of the Apuane Alps on the Versilian coast and the thick forests of the Apennine Range of Emilia-Romagna to the east. It is part of the province of Lucca and is characterised by deep valleys, covered in thick vegetation; twisting, free-flowing rivers and small, gritty towns. The Garfagnana has been inhabited since pre-historic times. The Liguri and Etruscans lived here before the Romans arrived, and it has always been a place of transit, criss-crossed by pilgrim trails and trading routes going north-south to and from Rome; and east-west to and from the rich towns on the Po River plain like Modena and Parma to the Tuscan coast. We recently spent a week exploring the Garfagnana, hiring a car and staying in a restored rustic cottage (with a swimming pool) above Bagni di Lucca on the Lima River. It was a fabulous holiday of exploring and surprises and, despite being the beginning of July, we hardly saw any other tourists during the whole week.'

        text: "\_Cutigliano is a handsome village twenty-six kilometres north, up the valley beyond Bagni di Lucca. It sits under Monte Abetone, the highest point on the old road between the Duchy of Tuscany with the Duchy of Modena. Because of its strategic position, Cutigliano was for several centuries the seat of the Captain of the Mountain. From Cutigliano, a cable car goes up to Doganaccia, which was a customs point between Tuscany and Modena. From here, a whole range of walking trails take you up above the treeline to the Cresta del’Olmo, 1574 mts, the ridge that separates the two regions. We took an easy 8 kilometre, out-and-back walk through forest and then up the ‘Casette della Valle’. Back in Doganaccia, we had a delicious lunch of mountain food at the Rifugio La Bicocca (tagliatelle with wild boar sauce!)."

        text: 'Barga is an eighteen-kilometre drive from Bagni di Lucca. It is considered one of the most beautiful villages in Italy. Because of its strategic position on the trade routes that linked Modena (and the Po Valley) with Tuscany, Barga has always been an important town. Barga became a trade town in the Middle Ages and exported silk to Florence. It is a pleasant place to while away time exploring its small lanes and squares. Because of high immigration in the 20th century, Barga has a very strong connection with Scotland and is officially ‘the most Scottish town in Italy’, with between 40 and 60% of its inhabitants with relatives in Scotland. There is a Sagra del Pesce e Patate (Fish and Chips Festival) in August to celebrate the connection and, yes, you can order a haggis pizza!'

It is an ancient place that was a staging point for pilgrims and traders making their way on this perilous road to and from the coast. In the 1950’s the town was abandoned to make way for a hydroelectric dam. Much of the village was submerged. In the 2000’s, many of the original inhabitants decided to reinhabit the parts of the village which survived the flood and there are now two very good restaurants operating here. The Osteria dell’Isola, is the fanciest, on the road, but I prefer the simpler Casa del Pescatore (House of the Fisherman), down overlooking the lake.         text: 'If you have the time and inclination, you can do a long, but very fascinating, drive back to Bagni di Lucca, via the coast. To do this you simply continue along the windy road that heads west over the mountains to Versilia and Tuscany. At a certain point the road forks and you have the choice of going right to Massa di Carrara or left to Forte dei Marmi. You are in the famous marble mountains of northern Tuscany now and either road will take you past spectacular opencut marble quarries, however, if take the road to Forte dei Marmi, you will pass under the quarries of Monte Altissimo, the most famous of all, opened by Michelangelo in the 1500s. You will also have the opportunity to visit the so-called ‘Occhio di Michelangelo’ (the Eye of Michelangelo), a striking marble rose window located on the facade of the nearby Pieve di San Martino, in the hamlet of La Cappella in Azzano. Legend attributes to the hand of the famous sculptor, who carved it as gift to the parish priest he had befriended. Once you hit the coast, it is a forty kilometres drive down the A11 autostrada to Lucca and then another twenty minutes back to Bagni di Lucca.'
